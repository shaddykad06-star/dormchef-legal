01 Weeks 1–2 Explore

Find food you’d actually make.

Browse the catalogue by what you can cook on and what you want to spend. Every meal carries an estimated cost per serving and a calorie count before you commit to it.

Then there are the communities, where the recipes come from students cooking in the same conditions you are. The best source of a cheap dinner worth eating is usually somebody one floor up who already worked it out.

02 Weeks 3–5 Plan

Put them on the days you’ll cook.

Lay the week out meal by meal. Each day totals its own calories as it fills, so the plan is readable at a glance before you commit to any of it.

Drag Thursday onto Tuesday. Eat the same thing three nights running if it was good. Leave Friday blank because Friday is not a cooking night and pretending otherwise is how plans die.

The planner does not moralise about any of that. It just totals what you put down, and when the week reads right, one tap turns the whole thing into a shopping list.

03 Weeks 6–8 Shop

One list, sorted by aisle.

Every ingredient from every planned meal, merged into a single list: duplicates combined, quantities added, grouped by where things sit in the store and priced item by item.

It reads in the order you walk: produce, then dairy, then the long middle aisles. Tick things off and the total comes down with you, so the number at the till is not a surprise you meet at the end.

Set a cap and the list is measured against it. It will suggest a cheaper swap where an honest one exists. It will not quietly delete an ingredient to hit a number, because a list that lies to you is worse than no list.

05 Weeks 13–16 Track

What you ate, and what’s left.

Meals you planned are already in the log, so cooking one and recording it is the same action. The dashboard runs the day against your calorie goal and keeps the rest of the list in reach.

The pantry runs the question backwards. Instead of telling you what to buy, tell it what is already on the shelf and it answers the only question that matters at the end of a term: what can I make right now, with this.

And if you cook something worth showing, show it. Follow the people whose food you would actually eat and pull any of it straight into your own week. Nobody has to see the failures.

Straight answers

The fine print.

No dates, no prices, no promises the app can’t keep yet.

What is DormChef?

Deciding what to eat, shopping for it and paying for it takes more work than the cooking does. DormChef handles that half. Pick or import meals you’d actually eat, and it works out what to buy and roughly what it’ll cost before you spend anything.

Does it work without a real kitchen?

Whatever you’ve got to cook on. The No-kitchen filter narrows everything to meals a microwave, kettle or rice cooker can handle, so a first-year with one outlet and a housemate with a full stove are both at home here. Vegetarian, vegan, high-protein and under-500-calorie filters run alongside it.

How does the grocery list work?

Plan your week and every ingredient is merged across recipes: duplicates combined, grouped by aisle, totalled for the week, estimates marked as estimates. Set a weekly budget and lists aim for it, without ever dropping an ingredient your recipes need.

Are the prices exact?

They are estimates, and the app marks them as estimates everywhere they appear. They are there to tell you roughly what a week costs before you commit to it, not to match a particular store’s shelf price on a particular day.

When can I use it?

It’s in development. Leave your email and you’ll hear as it progresses. The day it ships, you’ll know.
